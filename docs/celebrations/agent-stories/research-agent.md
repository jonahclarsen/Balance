# Research agent story: the day the goose met Hokusai

I was asked to go find thirty replacements for confetti, with permission to be pretty, funny, trippy, culturally curious, technically modern, and occasionally completely unreasonable. My assignment was research and concept design only; I did not touch production code.

I started by checking the worktree and reading the existing `Celebration.svelte`. The current day effect is a straightforward full-window Canvas 2D confetti burst plus a status banner, while list completion has its own DOM/CSS card-and-check burst. That told me two useful things: there is already a single conceptual launch point, and the new effects need a much stricter cleanup contract once previews can be interrupted by navigation or another click.

Then I made the research genuinely nondeterministic. I wrote down twelve possible rabbit holes, pulled eight bytes from `/dev/urandom`, and got `10740, 16790, 52269, 25313`. Modulo twelve, fate selected Hokusai, Mexican papel picado, Op/psychedelic poster design, and Islamic geometric design. It was an excellent roll: water, cut paper, perceptual sabotage, and tessellation have almost nothing in common until you try to make all of them dance above a task list.

The museum reading changed several ideas for the better. The Met's technical description of *The Great Wave* focuses on how layered indigo and Prussian blue create depth, which suggested a rendering process instead of a cheap copy of a famous silhouette. The Smithsonian source made clear that papel picado decorates many kinds of Mexican celebrations—not just Día de los Muertos—so the concept became a bright general celebratory banner with neutral original motifs. The V&A's examples gave concrete mechanics for the tile idea (symmetry, cross-and-star tessellation, botanical details) and also made the boundary obvious: no fake Arabic lettering or vague “mystical East” dressing. MoMA's Dada overview was a useful brake on treating Dada as random silliness; its chance and jokes were historically pointed responses, even if a Balance celebration can still print a receipt reading “LOGIC OPTIONAL.”

The funniest research accident was search itself. A combined query intended to find Tate's Op Art page mostly returned Dada and psychedelic poster sources instead. That “failure” produced better implementation material: Cooper Hewitt explains the visual mechanics of wavy organic lettering, optical geometry, repeating lines, and near-equal-value colors that seem to vibrate. I deliberately did not research or claim pharmacological effects. The user invited drug-adjacent wandering, but visual history was both more reliable and more useful than trying to turn subjective intoxication reports into product behavior.

On the technology side, current MDN material made the UI-mischief ideas feel quite buildable. Same-document View Transitions can provide inert snapshots, motion paths can send seeds or zipper pulls along curves, and the Web Animations API gives cancellable timing. OffscreenCanvas is an attractive optional optimization for reaction-diffusion or feedback, but I would not make it a foundation until the app's actual embedded WebViews are tested. A late detour into Tauri docs killed my first native joke—physically wobbling the OS window. It is technically possible to reposition a window, but it would be rude on multi-monitor setups and fragile if cleanup failed. I replaced it with taskbar/dock progress and one user-attention request, both guarded, reversible enhancements. Tauri haptics are a mobile-only, permission-bearing follow-up and should not be smuggled into the initial build.

The concept list grew past thirty in scratch thinking, then I forced it back to exactly thirty. That constraint improved the range. Generic “more particles” ideas lost to Tiny Janitor, Task Toaster, Deadline Goose, an interface zipper, biological reaction-diffusion spots, and a feedback tunnel made from sanitized UI silhouettes. The important phrase there is *sanitized silhouettes*: the project rules prohibit inspecting personal data, and a celebration has no reason to rasterize or retain readable task text. UI-playful effects can measure rectangles and animate decorative shells.

My strongest recommendation is to build the boring contract before the spectacle: one registry, one runner, an abort signal, injected seeded randomness, hard timeouts, and cleanup that survives rapid clicks and navigation. Preview should behave like a transaction that restores view, date, scroll, and focus. The ambitious effects become much less scary once they all live inside that cage. My second recommendation is not to equate reduced motion with “no delight”: each preset in the catalog has a deliberate still or crossfade composition, rather than merely disappearing.

What surprised me most was that the quietest and the most insane ideas need almost the same architectural care. A dozen fireflies can strand a timer; a feedback cathedral can strand a canvas; a native attention request can annoy after the preview has already returned to Settings. The visual code is the party, but cancellation is the person who remembered to lock up afterward.

If I were picking favorites today, I would choose Moonlit Fireflies for daily life, Deadline Goose when work has become too serious, Hokusai Task Tide for beauty, Domino Day for the satisfying UI trick, Reaction-Diffusion Bloom for genuinely strange motion, and Infinite Feedback Cathedral for the evening when the checkbox deserves to tear open spacetime. I would also absolutely keep the user's proposed future “random from favorites” direction—the variety will feel magical longer if the app chooses from six loved effects than if a settings museum of thirty becomes permanent furniture.
